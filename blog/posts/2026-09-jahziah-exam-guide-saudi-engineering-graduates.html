The Jahziah Test, Explained: What Saudi Engineering Graduates Face

September 23, 2026 · Engineering Education · 7 min read

Every term, the same question reaches me, usually in the corridor after a lecture or in office hours during the last weeks of the semester. A final-year engineering student wants to know whether the Jahziah test decides if they graduate, and what is actually on it.

I have answered a version of that question since I joined the University of Tabuk as a lecturer, a move I wrote about when I made it, and now as an assistant professor of mechanical engineering. The short answer surprises almost everyone who asks it. No, it does not decide whether you graduate. Yes, it is worth taking seriously anyway.

What students find online is thin. A university announcement lists the date. A forum thread repeats a rumour about how many questions there are. A practice site sells questions without ever explaining an answer. So I went back to ETEC's own 2026 student guide, read the parts that matter, and put the plain version in writing here. I also built a practice tool for this test, which I will get to and label clearly as mine.

What the Jahziah Test Actually Is

Jahziah is a standardized test run by ETEC, the Education and Training Evaluation Commission. It does not test a single course, and it does not test your memory of one professor's slides. It measures whether a program's expected graduates have met the program's learning outcomes. The unit being measured is the program, and the graduates are how ETEC measures it.

It runs across roughly 51 disciplines. The engineering versions are the ones I work with: mechanical, electrical, civil, industrial, and software engineering, alongside chemical engineering and architecture. Two things follow from the design.

First, it is standardized. The same content scope, format, and rules apply to every student in the same discipline across the country. Your test is comparable to the one sat by a student three hundred kilometres away, which is the whole point. Second, it is not a licence and it is not a gate. It is a national read on how well a program is doing, taken at the moment its students are about to leave it. If you want the fuller explanation of where it sits in the Saudi system, there is a longer piece here.

How Many Questions, How Long, and What Is on the Paper

The numbers come straight from the 2026 guide. The test carries 55 to 85 multiple-choice questions, depending on the discipline. It runs 90 to 120 minutes, and that window includes the questionnaire at the end. The questionnaire is not scored; it is there to gather feedback, not to test you.

Engineering discipline (2026) Duration
Electrical, civil, mechanical, industrial, chemical, architecture 120 minutes
Software and computer engineering 90 minutes

What the questions test is the part most students get wrong. The paper is mapped to the program's subject standards, the list of outcomes the degree is supposed to deliver. It is not mapped to one textbook, and it does not follow one course. That is why revising the modules you found hardest is not the same as revising the paper.

Then the detail that catches almost everyone. The Essential Knowledge Units, which are mathematics, physics, and chemistry, are not covered. ETEC states plainly that the standardized tests do not include them. So hours spent grinding calculus or first-year physics are hours spent off the paper, however uncomfortable that sounds.

For the mechanical paper, the eight graduate knowledge units are weighted close to evenly, between 10 and 14 percent each. That weighting is curriculum-faithful rather than an exam secret, and I have written the mechanical topic breakdown separately if you want the detail.

Test Day, and What Actually Happens

It is computer-based, in person, at your own university's computer labs. Sessions run inside official university hours, roughly 9:00 to 15:00. You are not travelling to a testing centre in another city, and you are not sitting a paper exam in a hall. You sit at a machine, in a lab you have used before.

Inside the platform, three things are provided: a calculator, the formulas you need, and scratch paper. You sign a non-disclosure agreement before you start. You can flag a question and return to it later. The test is one timed section, followed by the questionnaire.

Engineering is administered in English. The technical questions and their options are in English for every engineering discipline. If your degree was taught in English, this changes nothing for you. If it was taught mainly in Arabic, it is worth knowing a semester before the test, not during the week of it.

What It Does Not Decide

This is the part that defuses most of the anxiety. Registration is usually handled by your university, so most students are enrolled without doing anything themselves. Results do not affect your graduation. ETEC reports a readiness outcome for the program rather than a personal pass or fail standing between you and your degree.

So what is it for? It is a measure of the program, and, read that way, a signal for you. Two students can sit the same test and both walk out with the same degree. One of them leaves knowing which outcomes are solid and which are not, at the exact moment they are about to look for a job or start a master's. That is worth having. The short FAQ answers cover the registration and results questions students ask most.

The 2026 Cycle, and Why 2027 Is Not Published Yet

The 2026 cycle ran from 1 to 17 February 2026. That is the confirmed baseline, taken from the guide itself, and it is the safest thing to plan around until ETEC says otherwise.

For 2027, there is no student guide yet. ETEC has not published one, which means any 2027 date you see online right now is a guess dressed up as information. The honest position is to prepare to the 2026 format, which is what we actually know, and watch for the official guide before you plan anything around a specific week. The 2027 guide hub keeps the confirmed 2026 facts in one place and labels them as such, and the dates and schedule page has the full per-discipline timing.

How I Would Prepare If I Were Sitting It

Every year, a handful of students tell me they revised for weeks. When I ask what they revised, the answer is usually the courses they found hardest, not the outcomes the test is built on. Hard and tested are not the same thing. That single mix-up costs more than any gap in knowledge.

If I were sitting the test this cycle, I would do four things.

Start from the outcomes, not the textbook. Get the subject standard for your discipline, read the outcomes, and mark which ones you could actually explain to someone. That list, not the course catalogue, is your revision plan.

Use active recall over rereading. Answering a question and getting it wrong teaches you more than reading the same paragraph three times. The discomfort of retrieving an answer is the part that builds memory, and rereading avoids that discomfort entirely.

Space the sessions out. Six short sessions across three weeks beat one long weekend before the test, and the gap between sessions is doing real work, not wasted time.

Sit timed mocks in the real format. The format is part of the test. A student who has never answered 70 questions under a countdown will lose time to the clock, not to the content. Do at least a few full-length sets before the week of the exam.

How to Tell a Practice Bank Worth Using

Most practice material for this test falls into one of two buckets: questions scraped from unrelated exams, or questions written with no source at all. Four questions separate a usable bank from a waste of your last month.

  • Does it explain why each wrong option is wrong, not just mark the right one?
  • Is every question mapped to an official subject-standard outcome?
  • Does it state plainly that its questions are approximate, not real exam content?
  • Does it claim to be official? If it does, close it. Only ETEC is official.

A bank that cannot answer the first question is a quiz, not a study tool. You will learn the answer to that one question and nothing about the outcome behind it, which is the wrong thing to optimise for an exam that tests outcomes.

Why I Built This

I teach the students who sit this test. For a few years I watched them revise the wrong material and could not point them at anything that explained its answers, worked in Arabic and English, and did not pretend to be the exam itself. So I built it.

Jahziah is a practice tool for the five engineering disciplines, mapped to the ETEC subject standards, with an explanation for every option and timed mocks in the published format. It is independent and not affiliated with ETEC, and it says so on every page. You can use the practice tool here.

Building the thing turned out to be the easy half. What to do with it once it works is the harder question, and I wrote about that separately in What to Do After You Build Something That Works.

Final Thought

If you are sitting the Jahziah test this cycle, the useful move is to stop treating it as a threat and start treating it as a syllabus. It tells you exactly which outcomes your program is being measured on, which is more guidance than most exams ever give you.

Revise those outcomes, sit a few timed sets, and walk in knowing what the format will feel like. The result will take care of itself, and it will not decide whether you graduate. It will just tell you, honestly, where you stand.
