Dr.-Ing. vs PhD: What the German Engineering Doctorate Actually Means

July 20, 2026 · Career & Academic Advice · 9 min read

I moved to Germany to do a PhD. That is what I applied for, that is what I told my family, and that is what I spent the next years doing. Nobody sat me down at any point to explain that I would not be finishing with a PhD at all.

I finished as a Doktor der Ingenieurwissenschaften. Dr.-Ing. I had not heard the term before I arrived in Freiberg.

Since then I have been asked about it often enough to know it is not just my confusion. Engineering students, especially those arriving from Pakistan, India, Egypt and the Gulf, ask me some version of the same quiet question: is this a real PhD, or is it something slightly less than one that Germany gives to engineers? The question is usually asked carefully, because nobody wants to insult the degree they are about to spend four years earning. So let me answer it properly, including the part of the history that makes the answer obvious.

Short answer: Dr.-Ing. stands for Doktor der Ingenieurwissenschaften, Doctor of Engineering Sciences. It is the German engineering doctorate awarded by engineering faculties. It is not a lesser title than a PhD, and in German engineering contexts it often signals the field more clearly than the generic English label would.

Germany Does Not Have One Doctorate. It Has Several.

This is the piece that resolves most of the confusion. In the English-speaking world there is essentially one research doctorate, the PhD, and the field is written after it in brackets. Germany does the opposite. The doctorate is named for the faculty that examined the work.

Germany awards roughly 28,000 doctorates a year, more than any other country in the European Union. Spread across medicine, engineering, the natural sciences, law and the humanities, a single undifferentiated title would tell a reader almost nothing. So the system labels them.

Title Full name Awarded in
Dr.-Ing. Doktor der Ingenieurwissenschaften Engineering sciences
Dr. rer. nat. Doctor rerum naturalium Physics, chemistry, biology, geosciences, computer science
Dr. med. Doctor medicinae Medicine
Dr. phil. Doctor philosophiae Humanities
Dr. rer. pol. Doctor rerum politicarum Economics, political science

Read that table again with the anxiety switched off. There is no hierarchy in it. There is no general doctorate at the top and specialised versions underneath. Every one of them is a research doctorate awarded for an original dissertation defended before a faculty. The prefix says which faculty. That is all it says.

A German colleague reading "Dr. med." does not think "that is a medical variant of a proper doctorate." They think "that person defended a medical dissertation." The same applies to yours.

You Do Not Choose It. Your Faculty Does.

There is no menu. When I enrolled, nobody offered me a choice between a PhD and a Dr.-Ing., because no such choice existed. I was doing research in an engineering faculty, so the degree that faculty awards is the degree I was going to receive.

This matters for anyone currently worrying about it, because it removes the decision entirely. If you are in a mechanical, electrical, materials, civil or process engineering faculty at a German technical university, you will finish with a Dr.-Ing. Nothing you do differently changes that, and nothing you did wrong caused it.

One real exception. Some faculties in mathematics, computer science and the natural sciences do let candidates choose between Dr.-Ing. and Dr. rer. nat., depending on whether the dissertation leans applied or theoretical. If you are in one of those departments, this is the one moment the distinction is genuinely yours to decide. Everywhere else it is settled by the building you work in.

The Title Was Won in an Argument, Not Handed Out

Here is the part almost nobody knows, and it is the reason the "is it a real doctorate" question is already answered.

Until the end of the nineteenth century, German technical colleges could not award doctorates at all. Only the classical universities could. Engineers were trained, employed and respected, and they were still locked out of the academic system, because traditional universities argued that technical education was about applying knowledge rather than producing it.

The engineers fought that for decades. In October 1899, at the centenary celebration of the Königlich Technische Hochschule zu Berlin, they won. Kaiser Wilhelm II had signed the authorisation on 11 October, and on 19 October it was announced publicly: Prussian technical colleges could now confer doctoral degrees. By 1901 every other German state had followed, and the institution that is now TU Berlin awarded its first two doctorates, one in chemistry and one in metallurgy.

The classical universities did not concede quietly. A Latin name had been proposed for the new degree, Doctor rerum technicarum, which would have placed it alongside Dr. rer. nat. and Dr. phil. in the same Latin naming tradition. The University of Berlin rejected it. The compromise was a title in German rather than Latin, hyphenated: Doktor-Ingenieur.

So the hyphen in Dr.-Ing. is not a typographic quirk. It is the visible scar of an argument that engineers won on the substance and half-lost on the styling. The degree is different in name precisely because it had to be different in name for the older faculties to accept it. Its academic standing was never the part in dispute after 1899.

When someone asks whether a Dr.-Ing. is equal to a PhD, they are reopening a question that was settled 127 years ago, by people who cared about it far more than either of us does.

It Answers Three Questions Before You Speak

Now the practical side, which is where I have changed my mind over the years. I used to think of the title as a formality. I now think it is quietly one of the more useful things on my CV.

Written in full, Dr.-Ing. tells an informed reader three things at once, before you have said a word: this person is an engineer, this person holds a doctorate, and this person earned it in Germany. No English-language title compresses that much information into eight characters.

It happens at conferences, in first meetings, in LinkedIn messages. Someone sees the title, and the very first question back is not "what do you work on." It is "where in Germany did you do your PhD?"

That is a conversation that has already started, with someone who has already placed me correctly, and I did not have to introduce myself to get there.

That is a real advantage and it is worth understanding for what it is. The title does not make anyone assume the work was better. It makes them assume they know where the work was done and to what standard that system holds people. In materials and mechanical engineering, that assumption tends to help.

There is a scarcity point here too that runs against intuition. Doctorates are not evenly distributed across German fields. Around 85% of chemistry graduates and roughly three in four biology graduates go on to a doctorate, according to CHE's analysis of Federal Statistical Office data. In mechanical engineering the figure is about 25%, and in electrical engineering about 26%. An engineering doctorate is the uncommon path within engineering, not the default one.

What I Actually Write on a CV

I do not write it the same way everywhere, and I do not think anyone should.

In German and European contexts, and anywhere the reader is likely to recognise the system, I write Dr.-Ing. in full. That is where it does its work. In contexts where the reader has no reference for it, I often drop the suffix and write Dr. Faisal Qayyum. Nothing is lost. In English, Dr. is the standard form for every doctorate anyway, so the shortened version is not a compromise, it is just the local convention.

The judgement is simple. Use the full form where it communicates something, and the short form where the full form would only prompt a question you did not intend to raise.

One place not to improvise. On formal documents, write the degree exactly as it was awarded. Credential evaluations, immigration files, university appointment paperwork, and professional registrations all check the designation against your certificate. "PhD" written where the certificate says "Dr.-Ing." is the kind of small mismatch that turns into weeks of correspondence. Informal shortening is fine. Formal substitution is not.

Nobody Has Ever Told Me It Counts Less

I want to be precise here, because this is the part people actually want to know and I can only speak from what I have seen.

In more than ten years of carrying this title, across academic positions in Germany and Saudi Arabia, conference circuits, journal review work, and consulting with industry, not one person has ever suggested to me that a Dr.-Ing. is worth less than a PhD. Not a hiring committee, not a reviewer, not a collaborator, not a client. I have never heard it second-hand from a colleague either.

That is experience, not a guarantee. I cannot tell you how a particular ministry, employer or credential evaluator in a particular country will process the designation on paper, and I would not want you to plan around my assurance instead of checking. If you are returning to a country with a formal equivalence process, ask that body directly and get the answer in writing.

What I can tell you is that the fear is far larger than anything I have encountered in reality. The people worried about this are almost always the people holding the degree, not the people reading it.

When the Distinction Should Affect Your Decision

Most of the time this should change nothing about what you do. Here is the filter for the times it should.

If your faculty offers you the choice between Dr.-Ing. and Dr. rer. nat., pick based on where you intend to work, not on which sounds more impressive. German industry tends to read Dr.-Ing. as the closer fit. Theory-heavy academic communities in the natural sciences often lean the other way. Both are the same level, so choose the signal that matches your destination.

If you are choosing between a doctorate in Germany and one elsewhere, the title should carry almost no weight in that decision. The supervisor, the funding, the group, and whether the work genuinely interests you will determine the next ten years of your career. The letters afterwards will not. If that decision is still open for you, PhD vs Industry in Germany deals with the question underneath it, which is whether you should be doing a doctorate at all.

If you are worried about recognition in a specific country, stop reasoning about it and go and check. One email to the relevant evaluation body answers definitively what a week of forum reading will not.

If you are already enrolled in an engineering faculty in Germany, there is nothing to decide. Finish the thesis. That is the part that matters and the part people will actually ask you about.

Final Thought

I did not know what I was going to be called when I got on the plane. That turned out to be entirely unimportant, which is the honest summary of this whole topic.

The title on the certificate is a label describing which faculty examined your work. It is not a grade, not a ranking, and not a judgement on whether you belong in the room. Germany chose to be specific where the English-speaking world chose to be general. Neither choice says anything about the quality of the research underneath it.

If you are two years into an engineering doctorate in Germany and quietly worrying that you are earning a second-tier version of a PhD, you are spending energy on the one part of this that was settled in 1899. Spend it on the thesis instead. Nobody has ever asked me what my title stands for. They ask what I worked on.
